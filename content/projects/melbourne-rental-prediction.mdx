## The problem

Airbnb hosts often need to choose a starting price before they have enough booking history to understand demand. This project turns historical listing data into a pricing model that can support that decision with a data-driven estimate.

## The data

The data contains Melbourne Airbnb listing records with structured, categorical, date, text and multi-item fields: location, property characteristics, host profile, availability windows, review scores, amenities and free-text fields.

The training data contains 7,000 rows and 61 columns. The test data contains 3,000 rows. The work included parsing currency and percentage strings to numerics, splitting free-text `bathrooms` into a count plus shared/private flags, converting `host_since`, `first_review` and `last_review` to age-in-days against a `2022-09-09` reference date, deriving amenity count and 16 amenity indicators, and rare-category grouping before one-hot encoding.

<Insight title="Feature engineering was the first model">
  Parsing currency strings, splitting free-text bathroom fields and deriving
  amenity indicators took the raw listing from 61 mostly-string columns into a
  numeric feature space the models could actually use. The derived
  host-account-age and amenity-count features mattered as much as the raw
  fields.
</Insight>

## The approach

The project used two-stage selection. Five models — Ridge, Elastic Net, Random Forest, Extra Trees and Histogram Gradient Boosting — were benchmarked on a 2,000-row subset. The top three were then validated on the full 80/20 split before the winner was trained on all rows.

  <img
    src="/projects/melbourne-rental-prediction/model_selection_comparison.png"

  />
  <figcaption className="mono text-muted mt-3 text-sm">

    split.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The final pipeline uses an `AirbnbFeatureEngineer`, numeric and categorical imputation, scaling, one-hot encoding with rare-category grouping, Histogram Gradient Boosting and a `log1p` target transform via `TransformedTargetRegressor`.

## Results

Histogram Gradient Boosting won on every metric. The gap to the linear baselines is stark: roughly 12% better RMSE and 14% better MAE than Elastic Net.

<Insight title="The R² of 0.248 is honest, not disappointing" tone="warning">
  The price distribution is heavily right-skewed and a handful of extreme
  listings dominate squared error. That is exactly why the p99-excluded metrics
  (MAE 50.20) are reported beside the full-validation figures — a single average
  would hide what the model actually does well.
</Insight>

## What I would do differently

- I would keep the honest p99-excluded metrics beside the full validation metrics when discussing model usefulness.
- I would continue to test how the skewed target distribution affects the business interpretation of a nightly price estimate.
