## Executive summary

A raw tweet corpus is not usable by a comms or social-listening team until it
is cleaned, indexed and ranked. This project turns 10,000 tweets stored in
MongoDB into searchable text intelligence through a five-stage pipeline,
parallelising the genuinely parallel stages with MapReduce and DASK rather
than distributing the whole thing for its own sake.

<Insight title="Key takeaway">
  The word-count stage found 22,157 unique word stems across the corpus, and
  Perth was the single most frequent city at 363 tweets — the only geography
  figure verified precisely enough to report on its own.
</Insight>

## Business problem

A comms, marketing or social-listening team cannot query or trend a raw tweet
dump. The decision this pipeline supports: **turn unstructured text into a
structure a downstream search index or dashboard can consume** — word
frequencies, geography, and ranked keywords, rather than a flat table of
tweet text.

## Approach

<PipelineFlow id="tweet-pipeline" title="Five-stage pipeline" />

1. **Clean and enrich** — tokenise, strip URLs, numbers and non-alphabetical
   characters, remove stop words, stem to root form, extract keywords, tag
   named entities via `nltk.pos_tag`, model topics with a gensim
   bag-of-words model, and score sentiment with `SentimentIntensityAnalyzer`.
2. **Word frequency** — MapReduce word count.
3. **Geography** — MapReduce count by city, with explicit fallbacks for
   no-location, non-Australian and no-city records.
4. **Sorting** — tweets grouped by id and sorted, implemented once with
   MapReduce and once with a hand-written merge sort, then timed against
   each other.
5. **Keyword ranking** — TF-IDF with scikit-learn, then sorted in parallel
   across a 100-partition DASK dataframe.

<Insight title="Key takeaway">
  The design parallelises the genuinely parallel stages (word count, geography,
  sorting) with MapReduce and DASK, while scikit-learn owns the TF-IDF
  computation itself. That boundary keeps the engineering honest — the whole
  pipeline is not oversold as distributed.
</Insight>

## Results

The word-count stage found **22,157 unique word stems** across the corpus.
Perth was the most frequent city at **363 tweets**. The MapReduce-vs-merge-sort
timing comparison was run during development but is not reported here because
it has not been independently re-verified — see What I'd do next.

## Recommendation

<Insight title="For the business" tone="success">
  This pipeline is the groundwork for a searchable tweet index or a geographic
  trend dashboard — the keyword ranking stage is the natural hand-off point to a
  downstream analytics or reporting tool, rather than an endpoint in itself.
</Insight>

## What I'd do next

- Re-time the MapReduce and merge-sort comparison properly before quoting it
  as a benchmark.
- Extend the geography stage beyond a single verified top-city figure with a
  full, re-run city breakdown.
- Wire the TF-IDF output into an actual downstream search index to prove the
  hand-off point in practice.

### Explored: a proper timing harness — with real (synthetic) benchmarks

<Insight title="Timed, and honestly labelled" tone="success">
  The sort comparison was run for real with a repeated `timeit` harness at
  10,000 synthetic items: **MapReduce-style partition/sort/merge 4.56 ms, merge
  sort 20.58 ms, Python `sorted()` reference 2.36 ms** (medians), with a scaling
  curve across corpus sizes. The benchmarks are explicitly **synthetic** — the
  original 10,000-tweet MongoDB corpus requires `MONGO_URI`, tracked as a
  blocker — and the existing corpus charts (top cities, top TF-IDF keywords)
  remain the evidence from the real run. Full details in the [next-steps
  report](https://github.com/Bibententen/mapreduce-dask-tweet-pipeline/blob/master/REPORT_UPDATE.md).
</Insight>
