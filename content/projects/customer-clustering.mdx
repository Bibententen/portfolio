## Executive summary

A small clothing retailer had customer records but no segmentation — every
customer got the same range, at the same price point, regardless of who they
were. This project clusters 2,000 customer records into three segments on age,
income, marital status, education, occupation and settlement size, then
identifies which segment the next collection should be designed and priced
around.

<Insight title="Key takeaway">
  Two independent clustering methods — K-means++ and agglomerative clustering —
  converge on the same three groups. That cross-method agreement, not either
  algorithm alone, is why the segmentation is trustworthy enough to act on.
</Insight>

## Business problem

The retailer's decision was concrete: **which single customer segment should
the next collection be designed and priced around?** Designing for "the
average customer" ignores that a retailer's base is rarely one homogeneous
group — a range built for the wrong segment sells slowly and gets marked down.

  age, which is what clustering does next.

## Approach

Cluster count was chosen with an elbow analysis on inertia, cross-checked
against agglomerative clustering — both converged on **three** segments. Two
algorithms were then run independently and compared:

- **K-means++** (`n_clusters=3`) on the scaled numeric and encoded categorical attributes.
- **Agglomerative clustering** (`n_clusters=3`) as an independent cross-check.

Both methods produced the same three groups under different labels, which is
the strongest evidence available that the segmentation reflects real structure
in the customer base rather than an artefact of one algorithm's assumptions.

## Recommendation

<Insight title="For the business" tone="success">
  Design and price the next collection around the **Mid segment** — the group
  with the disposable income (~$130k) and lifestyle profile (single, skilled,
  city-based) most likely to convert. Use the Lower segment's profile to justify
  a secondary value line, and the Upper segment (smallest group, by far the
  highest income) to justify a limited premium line rather than a core range.
</Insight>

## What I'd do next

- Get the real per-_k_ inertia values recorded so the elbow curve can be shown
  as a chart rather than described in prose.
- Compute a real agreement metric (e.g. adjusted Rand index) between K-means++
  and agglomerative clustering, rather than relying on visual label matching.
- Validate the segments against actual purchase or return data, since this
  clustering is based on demographic attributes only, not observed behaviour.

### Explored: the cross-check, made quantitative

<Insight title="From qualitative agreement to a number">
  K-means++ and agglomerative clustering were reported as agreeing on three
  segments. The Adjusted Rand Index turns that into a single agreement score
  between -1 and 1, computed directly from the two label sets already produced
  in this analysis — see
  [`notebooks/customer-clustering.ipynb`](https://github.com/Bibententen/customer-clustering-project/blob/master/customer-segmentation.ipynb).
  The score itself isn't quoted here, because it needs to be computed against
  the real 2,000-record dataset, which isn't shipped in this repository.
</Insight>
