## The problem

The task was to classify a biopsy as benign or malignant from tumour measurements. Prostate cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer in Australia (~24,200 male diagnoses in 2022) and has one of the highest survival rates when caught early. That makes a false negative much worse than a false positive.

## The data

The dataset contained 100 biopsies and 10 variables: radius, texture, perimeter, area, smoothness, compactness, symmetry, fractal dimension, id and the diagnosis label. There were 62 malignant and 38 benign records.

Missing values were present. Every predictor was numeric, so missing values were filled with the column median. The strongest predictors were perimeter, with 0.60 correlation with diagnosis; area, with 0.53; and compactness, with 0.51.

<Insight title="Three predictors carried the signal">
  Perimeter (0.60), area (0.53) and compactness (0.51) correlated most strongly
  with the diagnosis. All three are size-and-shape measures of the tumour —
  consistent with what clinicians expect: larger, more compact masses are more
  likely malignant.
</Insight>

## The approach

The work used a 70/30 train/test split and compared three classifiers: decision tree, random forest and XGBoost.

## Results

XGBoost was best at 80% accuracy. The confusion matrix tells the story the accuracy figure hides:

On a 100-row medical dataset with a 62/38 class split, 80% accuracy is a weak claim. The 3 false negatives are the number a reviewer should ask about because a false negative is the expensive error here.

<Insight title="80% accuracy is not the headline" tone="warning">
  With a 62/38 class split, a naive always-malignant classifier would already
  hit 62% accuracy. The meaningful numbers are the errors: 3 false negatives
  (missed cancers) and 3 false positives. For a medical use case, tuning the
  threshold toward recall would trade some false positives for fewer missed
  cancers — the right trade.
</Insight>

## What I would do differently

- I would tune the classification threshold towards recall.
- I would use stratified cross-validation instead of a single 70/30 split.
- I would collect far more data.
