## Executive summary

A biopsy classifier is only useful if it fails in the cheap direction. This
project classifies 100 prostate biopsies as benign or malignant from ten
tumour measurements, and the headline number to act on is not the 80%
accuracy — it's that the model produced exactly 3 false negatives (missed
cancers) against 3 false positives (unnecessary follow-ups) on its held-out
test set.

<Insight title="Key takeaway">
  Perimeter, area and compactness — all measures of tumour size and shape — are
  the strongest predictors, consistent with clinical intuition that larger, more
  irregular masses are more likely malignant.
</Insight>

## Clinical / business problem

Prostate cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer in Australia (~24,200
male diagnoses in 2022) and has one of the highest survival rates when caught
early. That makes the cost of the two error types asymmetric: **a false
negative delays treatment of a real cancer, while a false positive costs a
follow-up biopsy.** The decision this model informs is which biopsies get
flagged for urgent follow-up — so the error profile matters more than the
overall accuracy figure.

## Approach

The work used a 70/30 train/test split and compared three classifiers —
decision tree, random forest and XGBoost — selecting on held-out accuracy
before inspecting the error profile in detail.

## Results

XGBoost was the best of the three classifiers at 80% accuracy. On a 100-row
dataset with a 62/38 class split, a naive always-malignant classifier would
already score 62% — so the accuracy figure alone is a weak claim, and the
error counts are the meaningful result:

<Insight title="The errors that matter" tone="warning">
  30-sample held-out test · 24 correct (80%) · 3 false negatives (missed
  cancers) · 3 false positives. The full true-positive/true-negative split
  within those 30 samples is not recoverable from the reported figures, so this
  case study reports the verified stat-pair rather than a guessed confusion
  matrix.
</Insight>

## Recommendation

<Insight title="For the business" tone="success">
  Because a false negative means a missed cancer, tune the classification
  threshold to trade some false positives (unnecessary follow-up biopsies) for
  fewer false negatives, rather than optimising for raw accuracy. Precision and
  recall, not accuracy, should be the metrics reported to a clinical
  stakeholder.
</Insight>

## What I'd do next

- Tune the classification threshold towards recall rather than accuracy.
- Use stratified cross-validation instead of a single 70/30 split, given the
  dataset is only 100 rows.
- Collect more biopsies — at this sample size, a handful of borderline cases
  can move the accuracy figure by several percentage points.
